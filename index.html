Neutron-2 — my contributions

DESIGN · ANALYSIS · PEOPLE

My work on Neutron-2 connects the spacecraft’s integrated design, structural and thermal behavior, and the engineers developing it. I progressed the CAD through five design reviews, built a high-fidelity thermal model for CDR, and gained structural FEA experience while mentoring the next generation of the team.

01 / SPACECRAFT DESIGN

One spacecraft. Five design reviews.

I developed and progressed the integrated spacecraft CAD through SRR, SCR, PMR, PDR, and CDR. This work carried the spacecraft configuration through successive reviews, connecting individual hardware elements to the complete 3U assembly.

  1. 01SRR
  2. 02SCR
  3. 03PMR
  4. 04PDR
  5. 05CDR

Bringing the hardware together

The layout shows flight computing, attitude determination and control, power, communications, and neutron detector modules within the 3U structure. The payload computer and detectors sit alongside the supporting spacecraft systems, making their physical relationships visible in one model.

Continuity through the reviews

My contribution was to advance this integrated CAD across the full review sequence. The model provides a shared reference for discussing the spacecraft configuration and how its subsystems fit together as the design develops.

02 / THERMAL ANALYSIS

A high-fidelity model for CDR.

I built the high-fidelity thermal model for the Critical Design Review. It adds a thermal view of the spacecraft to the mechanical configuration, showing how temperature is distributed across the modeled hardware.

From configuration to thermal behavior

The visualization shows the modeled structure and internal hardware as a temperature field. The spatial differences provide a way to discuss thermal behavior at the component level while retaining the context of the complete spacecraft.

Prepared for the design review

The CAD and thermal model document complementary parts of my CDR work: the integrated hardware configuration and the thermal representation I created for review.

This view captures the model’s temperature distribution at a simulation time of 11,700 seconds.

03 / STRUCTURAL FEA

Studying the structure under load.

My Neutron-2 work also includes finite element analysis (FEA) of the 3U spacecraft structure in SolidWorks. This experience connects the CAD geometry with the frame’s response to applied loads and constraints, adding a structural perspective alongside my thermal analysis. The static study shows how displacement varies across the modeled frame.

The deformation is magnified for visualization. The color scale shows resultant displacement in millimeters.

04 / LEADERSHIP & LEAN

Developing the team behind the spacecraft.

Alongside the engineering models, I am implementing LEAN within Neutron-2 to support how the team works and learns. My focus is mentoring and training younger engineers as they take on spacecraft development responsibilities.

Technical leadership and mentorship

Developing a spacecraft also means developing the people who will carry the work forward. I connect my engineering role with the responsibility to mentor younger team members and help them build the knowledge needed to contribute to the project.

LEAN as a learning practice

My purpose in introducing LEAN is to support both project execution and engineer development. Mentoring and training are central to that effort, so that improvements in how we work also strengthen the team’s capability.

THE THREAD THROUGH MY WORK

Integrated design, structural and thermal analysis, and team development are connected responsibilities. My contribution is to carry the technical work forward while helping younger engineers grow into it.

AERIS — my contributions

COORDINATING THE MISSION AND THE TEAM

As Chief Engineer from January through December 2025, I coordinated team meetings and mission-level discussions to keep technical work, priorities, and deliverables aligned. I also trained team members in engineering software and managed license use, supporting a student-led team responsible for planning and carrying out its own work.

01 / AERIS: SPATIUM & HARDWARE

Bringing the mission onto the workbench.

Through my AERIS: SPATIUM internship, I built the Artemis CubeSat Kit as a flatsat for the mission. Working with the kit in a bench-top arrangement gave me practical experience with the hardware and its connections, complementing the mission-level coordination I carried out as Chief Engineer.

Learning from the hardware

Assembling the flatsat helped me connect individual components to the spacecraft’s larger functions and encounter the kit’s limitations firsthand. Those practical challenges became the starting point for my proposal to upgrade the Artemis Kit through Callisto-Sat.

Connecting assembly and architecture

The flatsat and the systems diagrams offer complementary views of the mission: one makes the hardware accessible on the bench, while the other makes the relationships between subsystems explicit. Together, they connect hands-on work with a system-wide understanding of the spacecraft.

02 / MBSE & SYSTEMS DIAGRAMS

Making the subsystem connections visible.

I used model-based systems engineering (MBSE) in Capella to create diagrams of AERIS’s subsystem functions and interfaces. These views bring attitude determination and control, onboard computing, electrical power, communications, and the science payloads into a shared architecture, making it easier to discuss how the mission’s parts work together.

Functions and data flow

This view organizes what each subsystem does and how information moves between them. Commands, attitude information, telemetry, and payload data connect the subsystem functions, with the onboard computer coordinating exchanges across the spacecraft.

Power and communication interfaces

The interface view adds the connections needed to support those functions. It identifies power paths and communication links—including UART, I²C, GPIO, and SPI—so hardware relationships can be discussed alongside the system’s intended behavior.

Hawaiʻi Space Grant Consortium — my contributions

CALLISTO-SAT

Evolving the Artemis CubeSat Kit.

I am developing Callisto-Sat to upgrade the Artemis CubeSat Kit with a SatNOGs COMMs radio and model-based systems engineering (MBSE) manuals. The goal is a more capable, better-documented platform that future student teams can build on as the kit advances toward a higher Technology Readiness Level (TRL).

Development goal: TRL 6 → TRL 7

01 / COMMUNICATIONS & INTEGRATION

Making the radio part of the system.

My communications work connects the SatNOGs COMMs radio to the Artemis platform. Alongside the hardware integration effort, I have worked on the F′ flight-software interface for SPI communication between the radio and the onboard computer. This brings software development and electronics into my systems engineering work.

The radio interface work is part of an ongoing integration effort. Validation with the radio physically connected to the onboard computer, followed by system testing, remains part of the path toward higher readiness.

02 / MBSE & KNOWLEDGE TRANSFER

A platform future teams can understand.

The upgrade also addresses how students learn and use the kit. I am developing MBSE manuals and a reusable Capella library to organize the system architecture into connected models, giving future teams a structured starting point for their own missions.

Connected system models

The library captures subsystem functions and their relationships so the kit can be understood as a complete system. It supports a model-based approach to mission development, with related engineering information organized around a shared architecture.

Documentation that carries forward

The manuals and library are intended to help new cohorts understand the platform, adapt it to their needs, and refine the models as they learn. The library is still in development, with reuse and future release as part of its purpose.

03 / WHERE THE UPGRADE BEGAN

AERIS: SPATIUM

Before Callisto-Sat, I built the Artemis Kit as a flatsat for the AERIS mission through SPATIUM. Working with the kit in a bench-top arrangement gave me direct experience with its limitations and the practical challenges of using it for a mission.

FROM HANDS-ON EXPERIENCE TO A RESEARCH DIRECTION

That experience led me to propose the Artemis Kit upgrade. Callisto-Sat carries those lessons forward through improved communications capability and clearer MBSE documentation, with the aim of making the platform more useful for the student missions that follow.

02 / WHEEL DESIGN

Designing a part of the whole rover.

Wheel design was one of my main mechanical contributions. I worked on the wheels as part of the rover’s overall assembly, connecting the geometry of an individual component with the vehicle it would support.

Connecting component and assembly

The CAD view places the wheel geometry within the complete rover, while the lab photograph shows the wheel hardware alongside the chassis. These views capture the two scales of my mechanical work: designing a component and helping build the vehicle around it.

A foundation for later engineering work
